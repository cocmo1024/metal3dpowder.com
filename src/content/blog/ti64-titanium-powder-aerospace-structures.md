Ti64 remains the default aerospace AM powder conversation because it solves a very specific structural problem well: high specific strength in geometries that are difficult to machine or assemble efficiently. That does not make it the answer for every flight part. It means it is often the first serious alloy to evaluate when weight, geometry consolidation, and structural credibility matter at the same time.

## Where Ti64 is usually the right aerospace route

Ti64 becomes practical when the part is:

- weight-driven
- structurally loaded
- geometry-constrained
- difficult to machine as one piece

Typical examples include brackets, housings, supports, manifolds, and other structural hardware where LPBF or EBM can remove fasteners, reduce part count, or improve the stiffness-to-weight result.

## Why aerospace teams stay with Ti64 so often

The material is widely understood, broadly processable, and compatible with the kind of qualification mindset aerospace teams already expect. It is not just the alloy properties. It is also the maturity of the surrounding manufacturing route.

That matters because aerospace programs do not adopt AM materials for novelty. They adopt them when the geometry value and the process discipline justify the transition.

## Where Ti64 is less convincing

Ti64 is not automatically the best answer when the part is:

- primarily corrosion-driven rather than structural
- dominated by extreme hot-section service
- easily machined from wrought stock without part-count penalty

In those cases, the geometry benefit or service condition may point toward a different route. The key decision is not whether titanium is attractive in general. It is whether Ti64 is the right structural titanium route for the exact use case.

## Process fit matters as much as alloy fit

For aerospace structural parts, Ti64 is most often discussed in LPBF, but EBM and DED also enter the picture depending on section size, residual-stress strategy, repair logic, or deposition rate needs.

That is why the application decision should always answer two questions together:

1. is Ti64 the right alloy family for the part
2. is the chosen AM route the right manufacturing path for the geometry

## The practical takeaway

Ti64 is strongest as an aerospace AM powder when the part is structurally loaded, weight-sensitive, and geometrically valuable enough that AM changes the design outcome. If the part is really a structural titanium problem rather than a generic metal problem, Ti64 usually deserves to be the first route reviewed. Satellite brackets, propulsion supports, UAV structures, eVTOL nodes, and hydraulic-support hardware are covered on this consolidated aerospace page; the RFQ should identify the actual load case, environment, interfaces, qualification stage, and inspection basis.

## One aerospace intent, several component classes

Drone landing gear, eVTOL airframe nodes, satellite brackets, lattice supports, and aircraft hydraulic blocks look like different search topics. For powder purchasing, they share one primary decision: whether Ti64 powder and a controlled AM route can deliver the required strength-to-weight, geometry, surface, and qualification basis.

| Component class | Why Ti64 AM enters the discussion | Main design or acceptance risk | Evidence the powder quote cannot replace |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| eVTOL or UAV brackets and landing-gear nodes | Low mass, integrated load paths, low-volume geometry | Fatigue, impact, fastener interfaces, build orientation | Structural allowables and representative fatigue testing |
| Satellite brackets and propulsion supports | Part consolidation and mass reduction | Launch load, thermal cycling, cleanliness, configuration control | Program-specific environmental and acceptance testing |
| Lattice or topology-optimized nodes | Material placed around a defined load path | Thin-strut defects, support removal, surface condition | Validated geometry-specific inspection and test method |
| Hydraulic manifolds or valve blocks | Internal routing and fewer joined interfaces | Trapped powder, leak paths, port machining, pressure boundary | Cleaning validation, pressure proof, leak test, and fluid compatibility |
| General aircraft structural hardware | Strength-to-weight and difficult machining | Anisotropy, defects, surface finish, fatigue-critical zones | Qualified powder-machine-parameter-post-process route |

These applications should not be published as separate pages merely by changing the industry noun. A separate page is justified only when the searcher faces a materially different acceptance decision. Hydraulic manifolds, for example, add internal-channel cleaning and pressure integrity; satellite brackets add program configuration and environmental testing. Those requirements now belong here as sections of the same aerospace powder decision.

## Powder specification for an aerospace Ti64 inquiry

Start with the exact alloy basis. “Ti64” can refer to Grade 5, Grade 23/ELI, TC4, or a project-specific chemistry. State the referenced chemistry, controlled interstitials, powder production route, virgin or reused condition, and supplier change-notification requirement.

The PSD must be tied to the machine route. A nominal 15-45 µm or 15-53 µm cut is not sufficient without the sampling and measurement method. Use the [LPBF particle-size guide](/posts/Alloys/lpbf-powder-particle-size-guide/) and [powder lot sampling guide](/posts/Alloys/am-metal-powder-lot-sampling-for-psd/) to define the reporting basis. For Grade 5 versus Grade 23 chemistry, use the [Grade 23 versus Grade 5 guide](/posts/Alloys/ti64-grade-23-vs-grade-5-powder/).

Request the lot-specific COA and the available PSD, morphology or SEM evidence through the RFQ. A representative marketing micrograph is useful for orientation but is not lot acceptance unless its sample, scale, method, and traceability are identified.

## Separate powder acceptance from flight-hardware acceptance

The evidence chain should remain explicit:

1. **Powder release:** identity, chemistry, PSD, morphology, contamination, condition, packaging, and traceability.
2. **Process control:** machine, parameter set, atmosphere, orientation, support, powder-reuse policy, and build monitoring.
3. **Post-processing:** stress relief, heat treatment, HIP where required, support removal, machining, and surface finishing.
4. **Part acceptance:** dimensional inspection, critical-zone NDE, mechanical or fatigue testing, pressure or leak testing where applicable, and configuration records.

Passing the first step does not prove the fourth. This distinction is especially important when an inquiry uses words such as “flight ready,” “aerospace grade,” or “satellite qualified.” Those terms need a stated program and acceptance basis, not a generic powder claim.

## RFQ fields for Ti64 aerospace powder

Provide the component class, Grade 5 or Grade 23 basis, process and machine family, target PSD and method, trial or production quantity, controlled interstitial limits, documentation package, reuse policy, destination, and schedule. Add the intended heat treatment, HIP or machining route when known. If the part contains internal fluid paths, include cleaning, pressure, and leak-test requirements. If it is fatigue-sensitive, state the orientation and coupon evidence expected from the manufacturing route.
