CoCrMo stays central in dental metal AM because it fits the actual application mix well: small geometry, repeatable workflows, and metal parts where wear resistance, corrosion behavior, and established dental processing routes all matter.

This is not just an alloy-familiarity story. It is an application fit story.

## Where CoCrMo is strongest in dental AM

CoCrMo is most relevant when the workflow centers on:

- dental frameworks
- bridge-related structures
- crown-support geometries
- other small precision dental metal parts

These are parts where dimensional control, surface finishing strategy, and predictable post-processing matter as much as the base alloy itself.

## Why dental labs keep returning to cobalt chrome

The answer is practical. CoCrMo offers a good balance of:

- established dental use
- mechanical credibility
- corrosion resistance
- repeatable lab-side familiarity

That makes it easier to adopt at scale than less familiar routes, especially when the workflow already depends on cobalt chrome finishing and handling habits.

## Where titanium can still challenge it

Titanium enters the conversation when the application or lab strategy has a real reason to prefer it. But that is an application decision, not a status decision. The question should always be which alloy fits the actual dental workflow better.

If that comparison is active, the direct cobalt chrome vs titanium dental guide is the right next step.

## What matters beyond the alloy name

For dental frameworks and related parts, alloy choice is only part of the result. The practical outcome still depends on:

- printer compatibility
- feature scale
- finishing path
- fit-sensitive geometry
- how the lab controls the repeat workflow

That is why CoCrMo is most useful when it is treated as a dental production route, not just as a powder label.

## The practical takeaway

CoCrMo remains central in dental AM because it matches the real workflow of frameworks, bridges, and related precision dental parts. If the application depends on repeatable lab-side metal printing rather than on generic material interest, CoCrMo is still one of the strongest routes to evaluate first.

## CoCrMo versus titanium in dental AM

The dental material decision should follow the restoration, device, clinical route, lab workflow, and governing requirements. CoCrMo and titanium are not interchangeable “medical powders.”

| Decision | CoCrMo route | Titanium route | Buyer question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Frameworks, bridges, and crown-related metal structures | Common candidate where stiffness, dental workflow, and finishing route fit | May fit a different restoration, implant, or lab route | What exact dental indication and material specification apply? |
| Implant or bone-contact hardware | Not established by a dental-framework powder offer | Grade 23 titanium is often reviewed for implant-related routes | Is this a restoration, instrument, implant, or patient-specific device? |
| Finishing and fit | Requires controlled support removal, machining and polishing | Also depends on geometry, surface and finishing | Which surfaces are printed, machined, polished, coated, or joined? |
| Regulatory and biological evidence | Device- and market-specific | Device- and market-specific | Which authority, standard, risk class, and validation plan govern? |

This page does not make a clinical recommendation. It helps a lab, manufacturer, or sourcing team define the powder and evidence request before the device-specific qualification work begins.

## Powder controls for a repeat dental workflow

Specify the exact CoCrMo designation and chemistry limits, powder production route, PSD and method, virgin or reused condition, contamination controls, sieving and blending rules, packaging, and lot traceability. Dental production often repeats small geometries across many builds, so powder genealogy and reuse policy should be visible rather than handled as an undocumented shop practice.

Ask how the supplier defines a lot, what the COA reports, whether morphology or SEM evidence is lot-specific or representative, and what change notification applies to atomization route, PSD classification, or chemistry. The [metal powder COA guide](/posts/Alloys/metal-powder-specification-guide-psd-oxygen-flowability/) provides the purchasing structure.

## Fit, support, and surface are finished-part questions

Powder flow or apparent density cannot prove crown margin, framework fit, surface condition, or repeatability. Those outcomes depend on build orientation, support strategy, layer parameters, compensation, heat treatment, support removal, machining, polishing, inspection, and the lab's digital workflow.

Define which dimensions and surfaces are critical, how fit will be measured, and whether coupons represent the actual thin dental geometry. A dense tensile coupon does not automatically validate a framework, bridge, or crown production route.

## RFQ fields

Include the dental application, exact alloy/specification, process and machine family, target PSD, powder condition, trial or production quantity, destination, required COA and traceability, and available TDS/SDS or morphology evidence. State whether the request is for development, routine lab production, or a regulated device program. Final device acceptance remains with the responsible manufacturer and applicable regulatory route.
